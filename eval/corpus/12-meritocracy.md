The thing nobody says out loud about meritocracy is that it was designed as an insult and we adopted it as an aspiration. Young coined the word in a satire, the whole book is a warning, and we read it as a product spec. I keep coming back to this because I think the joke he was making is coming true in exactly the shape he drew, and I want to work out whether I actually believe that or just enjoy believing it.

Here's the strong version of the case. The old aristocracy at least knew its position was luck. Born a lord, you might be cruel but you weren't confused, everyone including you understood that you'd won a lottery. The meritocrat has been told, by test scores and admissions letters and promotions, that his position is earned. Deserved. And desert is a solvent for solidarity. If I earned my place then the people below me earned theirs too, and helping them becomes optional charity instead of obligation. The meritocracy launders luck into desert, that's its actual product. The sorting is real enough, the deserving is the fiction.

And the luck-laundering runs deep, because even the traits that do the earning are unearned. I didn't choose to be born with whatever lets me sit still and take tests, into a family with books, in a decade where my particular knack got expensive. Rawls has the famous version of this, the natural lottery, even your work ethic came from somewhere. Push on desert hard enough and it's turtles all the way down, there's no floor where the earning starts.

Now, the counterattack I'd face at any dinner table: fine, but what's the alternative, sorting by merit beats sorting by birth or bribe, would you rather your surgeon were chosen by lottery? And this is where I have to be careful because the counterattack is correct as far as it goes. I don't want unqualified surgeons. Selection by competence is good. But notice the switch, competence-selection is a claim about who should do the job, desert is a claim about who should own the rewards. You can staff the operating room by skill and still not conclude the surgeon deserves twenty times the orderly's life. The meritocracy's trick is welding those two claims together so that questioning the second sounds like questioning the first.

So the position I'm landing on, and I think I do believe it, is that the problem was never the sorting, it's the sanctification of the sorting. Keep the exams, lose the theology. What that means concretely I'm less sure about, probably it cashes out as compressing the reward spread rather than reforming the selection, the surgeon still gets the scalpel, just not the moral halo and maybe not the entire pay ratio. The residue I can't resolve is psychological, whether people will run as hard without the halo, whether the fiction of desert is load-bearing for effort itself. Young thought the fiction would curdle into arrogance above and humiliation below. Looking around, the man had a point.
