I keep coming back to the idea that mass production killed craftsmanship, and I think I actually believe it. Everything in my apartment is disposable. The chair wobbles and there is no one to take it to, you just buy another chair. My grandfather fixed his own radio with a soldering iron and I throw away headphones because one side got quiet. Something real got lost there, not just objects but a whole relationship to objects, the assumption that a thing you own is a thing you maintain.

But I know the counterargument is sitting right there and I keep stepping around it. Mass production is also why ordinary people have chairs at all. The handmade things we romanticize were luxury goods, most families in the golden age of craftsmanship owned almost nothing. So when I mourn well-made objects, whose experience am I actually mourning? Probably some imagined gentleman's study, not an actual working household from 1900.

Still, I don't think that fully dissolves the point. Affordability and repairability are different axes. We could have cheap things that are also fixable, the fact that we mostly don't is a choice somebody made, glued cases and proprietary screws are decisions not physics. Planned obsolescence is a business model wearing the costume of progress. That's the version of the argument I want to make, not the nostalgic one. The nostalgic version is weak and I know it's weak. The design-choice version feels strong and I want to lean on it, the question is whether the economics actually support it or whether repairable-and-cheap is a fantasy combination, like I want the moral clarity without doing the arithmetic.
