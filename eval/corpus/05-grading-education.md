Grades are a lie we tell in both directions and after eleven years of teaching I'm ready to just say it. They lie to the student about what learning is, they lie to the institution about what happened in the room. A kid who came in unable to write a paragraph and left writing clear pages gets a C because the rubric doesn't have a row for distance traveled. A kid who could already do everything coasts to an A and learns exactly nothing except that coasting works. Whatever we're measuring, it isn't learning, it's arrival state, mostly determined by what happened before they ever met me.

And the perverse part is that everyone in the building knows this. Teachers know it, students definitely know it, admin knows it. The grade persists because it's cheap to produce and everyone downstream, colleges, employers, parents, wants a number they don't have to think about. It's a compression format. Lossy compression, and what gets lost is precisely the part that was education.

What I'd want instead is something like a narrative record, what can this person do now that they couldn't do in September, witnessed by someone who watched it happen. I know the objections, it doesn't scale, it's subjective, it invites bias, colleges can't sort ten thousand essays. Those objections are real and I don't have clean answers to all of them, especially the bias one, that one worries me more than the scaling one honestly. But we've optimized the whole apparatus around cheap sorting and just accepted that the thing being sorted stops being learning. That trade was made silently and I think if we made it out loud we'd be ashamed of it.
