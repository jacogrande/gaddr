I've become the person at the party who defends nuclear power and I want to write down why, partly to check that I still believe it when it's not a performance. The core of it is simple, we panicked about the wrong danger. Coal kills continuously, invisibly, lungs and rivers and climate, and nuclear kills rarely and cinematically, and human brains are built to fear the cinema and not the statistics. Chernobyl has a body count, a real one, but the fossil body count runs to millions and nobody makes documentaries with green geiger-counter sound effects about particulate matter.

So the safety argument against nuclear is, I think, mostly settled and mostly settled in nuclear's favor, per terawatt-hour it sits down with wind and solar. What's actually wrong with nuclear is different and almost never what people lead with. It's slow and it's expensive. We seemingly forgot how to build the plants, every western project doubles its budget and eats a decade, whereas solar got cheap the way electronics get cheap, on a learning curve nuclear never found. France built fifty reactors in twenty years once, standardized designs, one after another like sausages, so it's clearly not physics that stops us, it's that we build each one as a bespoke cathedral.

Which reframes the whole debate for me. The question isn't reactors versus windmills, it's whether we can make reactors a product instead of a project. If small modular designs actually deliver factory economics, nuclear matters enormously, firm clean power, no storage problem. If they don't, then my party argument is winning a fight that construction economics already lost, and being right about the risk perception stuff is just a consolation prize. I notice I want SMRs to work partly for tribal reasons at this point, which is worth being honest about.
