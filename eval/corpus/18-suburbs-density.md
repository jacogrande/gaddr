We moved out here two years ago and I'm ready to say the quiet part: the suburb is a machine for making everything I claimed to value slightly impossible. I said I valued community, there is no place here a person can walk to and encounter another person by accident. I said I valued the kids' independence, there is nothing they can reach without me driving, so their entire social existence passes through my calendar. I said I valued nature, and I have a lawn, which is nature the way a mall fountain is a river.

The design isn't neutral and that's the point I keep sharpening. Every cul-de-sac, every setback, every single-use zone is a decision that bodies shall move by car and life shall happen by appointment. We didn't choose isolation, we chose granite counters and a third bedroom, and isolation came bundled in the foundation. Nobody at the closing walks you through the ambient costs, the thousand small encounters per year you're forfeiting, the second car that isn't optional, the hour of chauffeuring that replaces the hour the kid would have wandered.

Now the honesty check, because I distrust conversion narratives including mine. People here mostly seem content. The neighbors like it. Maybe the density romance is its own delusion and I'd last six months above a bar on a loud street before writing the reverse essay. And the third bedroom is genuinely good, my mother stays in it. What I actually believe, stated carefully, is not that suburbs are wrong but that the menu was rigged, zoning made this the only affordable shape of family life across most of the country, and calling the outcome a preference is laundering a constraint into a choice. Build the missing middle, the row houses and corner stores, and then watch what people pick. My bet is the cul-de-sac loses more often than its defenders think. That's a testable bet, which is why I like it.
